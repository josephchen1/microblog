re: Mega Backdoor Roth

Oct 12, 2025

Let’s talk about the Mega Backdoor Roth! Note that only some employers allow for this as it’s 1) more expensive 2) more complex 3) more overhead in general 4) targeted towards higher earners only

Pleasantly surprising, United Airlines has this baked into our Fidelity 401(k) plan, which I, despite being a bit too low-earning to fully take advantage of it, will try it out.

What is a Mega Backdoor Roth? So, this is a retirement strategy that comprises of two features inside a 401(k) plan to get tens of thousands more dollars into Roth status (meaning it can grow tax-free FOREVER!), far beyond the traditional ~$7k Roth IRA limit.

Those two features, which again may not be available for all 401(k) plans, are after-tax 401(k) contributions and Roth in-plan conversion. The former is a third contribution type after traditional pre-tax and Roth. The latter is the ability to roll this after-tax money out to a Roth IRA while still employed. With both, one can execute the Mega Backdoor Roth.

A 2022 report by Plan Sponsor Council of America found that only 21% of 401(k) plans allow after-tax contributions, and that’s only one of two mechanisms needed for this to work. I’d guess only 10-15% of 401(k) plans fully enable this strategy.

IRS Limits So, let’s talk about limits. 401(k) contributions are known to have a ~$23,500 cap. However, that only applies to pre-tax and Roth 401(k) contributions only. There’s actually a further limit of ~$69,000 which includes pre-tax and Roth, as well as employer match and after-tax! This means you can contribute after-tax contributions to your 401(k) even after meeting the $23,500 pre-tax and Roth cap. You don’t necessarily have to wait until you reach that $23,500 cap to start doing after-tax contribution. It’s just that it’s best to fully take advantage of the $23,500 pre-tax/Roth contributions first as it is the most tax-efficient and allows for essentially free money w/ employer match. I personally plan to do a percentage for pre-tax and after-tax on each paycheck for now as to not need a mid-year manual adjustment. Fidelity also helps cut the contributions off if they are set to go over the limits, which further reduces needed headspace.

Flow Paycheck → After-tax 401(k) → Roth in-plan conversion (inside 401(k) plan) → Grows tax-free as Roth 401(k) → Leave employer → Rollover Roth 401(k) to personal Roth IRA (no deadline, but never hurts to do it sooner than later) → No RMDs, Lifetime Tax-Free Growth

Summary Even among the financially-literate, many think the annual Roth contribution limit is strictly capped at ~$7,000. But inside certain 401(k) plans, this Mega Backdoor Roth strategy allows the conversion of up to $40,000+ per year into Roth status… tax-free forever. It’s lowkey ridiculous, but hey, enjoy it while it lasts. As for me, I do not get paid enough yet to fully partake in it, but that’s a me problem, not a you problem :D1

  • 1 tokyo negi? aka long green onion or 4 reg green onions(white part only)
  • 2 green onion / scallion (for topping)
  • 16 oz thinly sliced beef (like ribeye)
  • 2 tbsp neutral oil
  • 4 tsp sugar
  • 2 tbsp soy sauce
  • 4 servings udon! (1000g frozen udon or 360g dry udon)

broth

  • 5 cups dashi (2.5 tsp hondashi then? usually i do 1 tsp to 1 cup for full base stuff like miso, but here we alr have lots of other salt/umami already)
  • 3 tbsp soy sauce
  • 2 tbsp mirin
  • 2 tsp sugar
  • 0.25 tsp diamond crystal kosher salt (j for consistency apparently, if morton salt, use like 1/8 tsp instead)

broth steps

  1. 5 cups dashi broth to a low boil for 1-2 min -> then season w/ the soy sauce, mirin, sugar, and fancy salt (cover w/ lid to keep hot & prevent evaporation) & turn off heat after 1-2 min

meat steps

  1. cut white part of negi into 1/2 inch diagonal slices
  2. cut 2 green onion into thin slices for garnish
  3. heat frying pan and when hot, add 2 tbsp oil + negi and saute until golden brown n tender
  4. then add the beef, cook until not pink, and now add the 4 tsp sugar and 2 tbsp soy sauce
  5. stir-fry until well coated and then remove from heat

final steps

  1. make and drain udon (typically like 1 min to reheat w/ boil for frozen udon)
  2. portion udon in bowls + pour hot broth into them
  3. place stir-fried beef into bowls + season them w/ green onions

tips

  • for broth, can add 1/2 tsp white soy (shiro shoyu) at end for cleaner, more delicate finish perhaps? never tried
  • can marinate beef beforehand by tossing it w/ 1 tsp soy + 1 tsp mirin + pinch sugar for 10 min before cooking
  • can deglaze pan w/ 1-2 tbsp of hot broth after cooking + pour back into broth
  • for more veggies, add bokchoy & napa (add after broth seasoning + simmer for ~5 min before turning off fire) or spinach (at end, blanch w/ hot broth for 15s until bright green/wilted then immediately portion)

how early should we arrive to the airport?

Oct 6, 2025

currently reading gad allon’s post about when to arrive at airports (typing as i go, so it’s not going to be the most organized/concise/well-written). he introduces this idea of the newsvendor problem where a newsvendor has to decide how many newspaper to stock each day w/o knowing exact demand. hitting average means high stockouts or excess inventory, and the proposed solution is choosing a quantile of the demand distribution determined by relative cost of each outcome.

so for this airport arrival, studies have found that security wait times are let’s say 20 minutes. to calculate the cumulative distribution of waiting time b/w arrival to gate (F_s), we do C_o / (C_o + C_u) with Co being cost of overage or cost per minute of waiting too long and with Cu being one-time penealty of being late. we then input into F_s L*, which is the optimal arrival buffer or the quantile of the distribution that balances those costs.

thus, averages are misleading as you’d likely be in the 90th-99th percentile where the lower range would be for leisure and higher range would be time-sensitive, high-premium travel. you, as a passenger, would likely not be in 50th percentile as underage cost can be ridiculously expensive as we see with my dear friend Hope who had to pay an exorbitant fare to take the next international flight out to make it back home in time for work a month or so ago. as for the overage cost, even if we assume $100,000 average annual income at ~$48/hr, the additional hour, let’s say, is marginal, especially considering time spent in lounges or browsing shops, which may reduce the perceived cost of overage.

rational travelers optimize to higher quantiles, not the mean, as cost of missing a flight would likely easily dwarf the cost of waiting, especially for international flights.

so, yes, on average, you don’t have to be 2 hours early. but the variance remains too high and the underage cost too daunting that the safest strategy is as the century-old newsvendor says: overstock. whether it be time or newspaper, you’d rather have extra on hand than none at a time of desperation.

for myself, i almost always arrive quite early at international airports abroad. i try to aim for at least an hour between post-security/immigration and boarding, allowing me some time to explore the airport, grab some drinks and maybe food at lounges (w/ priority pass [essentially only valuable abroad]), and prepare all my offline entertainment. for domestic flights, i try to aim to be past security right as we start boarding. there’s no valuable lounge access that i have, and i’m less intrigued by domestic airports. however, aiming for that has led me to arrive at airports like SFO in the middle of boarding, yet I still somehow made it through security & onto my international flights ontime. tbf, they were all cases of poor route time estimation by uber + unplanned traffic incidents + optimistic time buffers; thus, that’s why you aim for the 90th+ percentile, not 50th. anyways, as for airport dad, i’m more convinced he doesn’t want to suffer emotional and mental exhaustion of post-missed-flight drama and chaos rather than the financial damage.

gyudon (japanese beef rice bowl)

Oct 5, 2025

source/inspo: Just One Cookbook

gyudon is one of my favorite japanese comfort foods: thinly sliced beef and onions simmered in a sweet-savory sauce, served over hot rice. it’s quick, easy to make, and just so tasty.

ingredients (4 servings)

  • 1 onion (about 8 oz)
  • 2 green onions
  • 1 lb thinly sliced beef (ribeye / short plate is my go-to)
  • cooked rice
  • optional: pickled red ginger for topping

sauce

  • 1 cup dashi (~0.53 tsp hondashi)
  • 4 tbsp sake (or chinese rice wine / water)
  • 4 tbsp mirin (or 4 tbsp water + 4 tsp sugar)
  • 6 tbsp soy sauce
  • 2 tbsp sugar (to taste)

steps

  1. thinly slice onion + green onion (diagonally)
  2. in a pan (off heat), mix dashi, sake, mirin, soy sauce, sugar (ensure sugar & hondashi is mostly dissolved)
  3. add onions into the broth & spread evenly. then, place beef slices on top.
  4. cover, turn on fire and bring to a simmer on medium, then lower heat and cook 3–4 minutes. skim off foam/scum as needed/wanted!
  5. sprinkle green onion, cook 1 more minute. also, good time to add beaten eggs if that’s something you’d like. i’ve never tried tbh.
  6. serve over hot rice with sauce, garnish with pickled ginger if desired (me personally, not a fan).

tips

  • great w/ sides like miso soup or a small salad
  • add toppings at the end like toasted sesame seeds or seaweed?
  • if u want more veggies/greens, you can add bokchoy or napa cabbage w/ onions or spinach at the end and stir for 30-60 secs.

中銀膠囊大樓 Nakagin Capsule Tower

1970-1972 日本, 東京 Tokyo, Japan

Located in the heart of Tokyo, Kurokawa Kisho’s Nakagin Capsule Tower housed 140 self-contained units. The small apartments were designed for short-term stays by people who worked in the urban centre while living in the suburbs, reflecting new trends in how people moved throughout the city. The capsules were marketed as additional office space, secondary residences, or simple sites to recharge near the workplace. The tower was one of the few buildings realised as part of the 1960s Japanese architectural movement known as Metabolism. The name of the movement was a biological metaphor for how architecture could be designed with the ability to evolve, accommodating growth and changing societal needs. Attached to a concrete core service tower, the Nakagin capsules could, in theory, be replaced, which would extend the overall lifespan of the building. However, the building fell into disrepair in the early 2000s.

Despite numerous attempts to save it, it was demolished in 2022. Some capsules were carefully restored and collected by museums around the world, such as Unit A806 at M+.

The work may evoke affectless computer code but was in fact inspired by Miyajima’s experiences in the hospitals he frequented as a chronically ill child. The continual flashing and counting of numbers illustrate the Buddhist concept of samsara, the ceaseless cycle of reincarnation.
